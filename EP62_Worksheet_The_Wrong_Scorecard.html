Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This is the coaching session the episode prepared you for, written down. Four parts. Nine exercises. One complete system, built and tested, by the end of it.

This worksheet is not about Warren Buffett or anyone else's success. It is about the actual person you keep checking on, the actual feeling that follows, and the actual ruler you have been handed. Use real names and real moments. Vague answers produce vague change.

Do it in one sitting if you can, with about 90 minutes and no feed open. Give Exercise 2 the most time. Most advice treats comparison as a confidence problem. This worksheet treats it as a borrowed measuring system, which you can trace, and therefore replace.

There are no right answers, only honest ones. If something uncomfortable comes up, that is not weakness. That is data.

📏 The Ruler Built for Someone Else's Room — Hold This Throughout

Someone hands you a tape measure and asks you to measure your kitchen. The numbers come out wrong. Too narrow. Too short. So you decide something is wrong with your kitchen.

Then you notice the tape was built for a completely different room. Your kitchen was never wrong. This worksheet is how you find the tape, and build your own.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — who you compare yourself to, what you feel, and the exact ruler in your hand
  • Something traced — who handed you your idea of "enough," and the sentence you have been obeying since
  • Something separated — their public result from your private reality, and your real evidence from your borrowed verdict
  • Something committed — your own scorecard, a tested system for keeping it, and one person who will ask how it is going
Part One

Discover — Naming the Comparison, Tracing the Ruler

Before you can build your own scorecard, you need to see clearly whose you have been using, how it got its authority, and what it has been costing you.

1
The Comparison Map
Naming who you check on, and what happens in your body when you do.

Comparison rarely announces itself. It arrives as curiosity, inspiration, or a quick look. Most people can say they compare. Far fewer can name exactly who, where, and what it does to them.

Coaching Prompt

For each area below, name the person, type of person, or feed you check on most. Then name what you feel in the moment right after you look, and where you feel it.

Not what you think you should feel. Write the first name that came to mind. Anchor the feeling in the body: chest, stomach, shoulders.

2
The Root — Tracing Whose Ruler This Is
A four part trace. Take your time here.

Choose the one area from Exercise 1 costing you the most peace right now. This is the area you will work through for the rest of this worksheet.

2a — The Person Who Handed Me the Ruler
Coaching Prompt

When did you first learn what "enough" looks like? Who first showed you what a successful, worthy person is supposed to have done by now?

A parent, a sibling you were measured against, a teacher, a first boss, or a culture that made certain milestones feel mandatory by certain ages. Try to see them as someone handing you the only ruler they had.

2b — The Formative Moment
Coaching Prompt

Was there a specific moment when being compared, ranked, or measured against someone else first stuck? What did you conclude about your worth afterward?

This may be one sharp memory or a slow accumulation. Take your time.

2c — The Inherited Sentence
Coaching Prompt

If your ruler could speak in a single sentence, a rule about what you should have achieved by now, what would it say?

Write it exactly as it would sound out loud. It may never have been said to you directly.

2d — The Cost Tally
Coaching Prompt

When was the last time you chased something, got it, and felt strangely empty? What did that tell you about whose target it was?

Include achievements that brought less relief than expected, decisions made to look right rather than be right, and peace spent on other people's timelines.

Notice ThisThe person who handed you this ruler was very likely using the only one they had, in a room that was not yours. Naming it is not blame. It is the first honest step toward choosing your own.

The Highlight Reel Audit
Separating their public result from your private reality.

Comparison measures your behind the scenes against someone else's highlight. This exercise puts the missing half back into the picture.

Check the Distortions That Apply

Comparison bends reality in three quiet ways.

  • The illusion of equal starting lines. I am comparing my mile three to their finish line, ignoring what came before it.
  • The moving goalpost. Every time I reach a target based on their position, it moves when they do.
  • The loss of my own voice. I have started copying their tone, pace, or strategy, and muting what made my path mine.
"You can win someone else's race and still lose your own life."
Part Two

Define — Building Your Own Ruler

Part Two builds the three moves from the episode into a working system: measure your trajectory, audit your inputs, and replace the question you live by.

4
Move One — The Trajectory Ledger
Measuring who you are today against who you were twelve months ago.

Position is relational. Trajectory is personal. The only fair comparison is you, against you. Growth is not a ranking. It is a direction.

Coaching Prompt

6
Move Three — Your Own Scorecard
Replacing the question you live by.
Verified Story

Warren Buffett describes two ways of living. With an Outer Scorecard, you grade yourself by what the world will think. With an Inner Scorecard, you grade yourself by how you actually behave. He has said it helps if you can be satisfied with an Inner Scorecard, and he credits the idea to his father, Howard.

Source: Alice Schroeder, The Snowball: Warren Buffett and the Business of Life (Bantam, 2008).

An Inner Scorecard only works if it measures things you actually control. Build yours below.

Coaching Prompt

If nobody ever saw the result, and there was no one to compare it to, what would you still want to build, become, or protect?

Let the answer be simpler than you expected. And let it be yours.

Say your replacement question and redirect phrase out loud once. They should feel almost automatic, not something you have to think through.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing Your Scorecard Against Real Life

A scorecard survives easily on a calm afternoon. It does not survive as easily against a rival's big announcement, an old voice returning, or someone questioning your pace, unless you have already thought it through.

7
Scenario One — The Announcement
When someone else's big win lands on a day you were doing your own work.
The Scenario

It is the middle of a focused week. Someone you compare yourself to announces exactly the milestone you have been hoping for. Within a second, your own progress feels smaller, and you feel the pull to open their profile, then everyone else's.

This tests whether your Inner Scorecard holds when the Outer one is loud.
8
Scenario Two — The Old Sentence Returns
When a run of good weeks is interrupted by the inherited belief.
The Scenario

You have had several genuinely good weeks on your own terms. Then the exact sentence from Exercise 2c resurfaces, telling you that you should be further along by now.

This tests whether you can recognise the old ruler in a new outfit.
9
Scenario Three — Someone Hands You Their Ruler
When a person you respect questions your pace or your path.
The Scenario

Someone close to you asks why you are not further along, or urges you to copy the strategy that is working for someone else. It comes from care, but it is delivered on their ruler.

This tests whether you can hold your own scorecard without needing their agreement.
Part Four

Protect — Making This a Practice, Not a Moment

One honest afternoon is a strong start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns this worksheet into an ongoing practice, so the old ruler does not quietly return for months before you notice.

✓
The Audit Schedule and Accountability Protocol
The recurring practice that catches drift early, plus a relapse plan.
  • Daily: Say your replacement question aloud each morning before you open any feed.
  • Within 7 Days: Put your input audit decisions from Exercise 5 in place, with the mechanism you named.
  • Every Week: Ask one question. "Whose scorecard did I mostly use this week, mine or theirs?"
  • Every Month: Add one new line to your Trajectory Ledger. Evidence only counts if you record it.
  • Every Quarter: Retake Exercise 1 and reread your sentence from 2c. Has a new ruler started forming somewhere else?
  • Whenever You Catch Yourself Looking Sideways: Use the moment as proof the system works. Noticing is the win.

Before You Close This

The tape measure was never wrong, and neither was your kitchen. You were using a ruler built for someone else's room. What you control is which scorecard you carry, and how quickly you notice when you have picked up the wrong one.

Staying in your own lane is not about lowering your ambition. It is about protecting your focus and clarity, so your ambition is pointed at something that is genuinely yours. That is what Living in Full Expression looks like in practice.

Before you close this session, write the one thing you now know that you did not know when you opened it.

The feeds will still be there tomorrow. You now know whose ruler you were using, who handed it to you, and what your own looks like. The only thing left is to keep measuring your own room.
