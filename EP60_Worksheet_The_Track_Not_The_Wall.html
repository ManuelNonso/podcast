Before You Begin

How to Use This Worksheet

This is the coaching session the episode prepared you for, in written form, built to actually change what you do Monday morning, not just how you feel today. Four parts. Nine exercises. A full root-cause trace of why your eyes default to the wall, and one complete focus system, built and tested, by the end of it.

This worksheet is not about race cars or wedding photography. It is about the actual wall pulling your attention right now, in your family, your health, your business or career, or your own growth, and the actual track you want your eyes on instead.

Give yourself real time with Exercise 2 especially. Most focus advice treats distraction as a willpower problem. This worksheet treats it as a trained pattern, one you can trace, and therefore one you can retrain.

From Part Two onward, this worksheet branches into two paths, distraction that comes from outside you, and distraction that comes from inside you, because they are solved with different tools. Read both branches before choosing.

📸 The Photographer's Lens — Hold This Throughout

A wedding photographer cannot stop guests from moving through the frame. What they can control is where the lens is pointed, and how much visual weight everything else is allowed to carry. Even people who end up in the shot get consciously blurred, so the subject that matters stays sharp.

This worksheet is how you build your own lens, decide on your subject, and practice the blur, on purpose, in every part of your life where the frame has gotten crowded.

What This Session Will Produce
  • Something named — the specific wall pulling your attention, and the specific track you actually want to be looking at, in the area of life costing you the most right now
  • Something traced — the family pattern, formative moment, and inherited belief that taught you to watch danger instead of the goal
  • Something separated — monitoring an obstacle, from being steered by it
  • Something committed — one complete focus system, tested against real pressure, and practiced starting this week
Part One

Discover — Naming the Wall, Tracing Where You Learned to Stare

Before you can build real focus, you need to see clearly which wall is currently running your attention, how it got that power, and where you first learned that watching danger felt safer than watching the goal.

1
The Domain Scan
Naming the subject and the wall, across every area where focus is currently being lost.

Most people can name that they're "distracted" in general. Far fewer can name, precisely, what their eyes are actually on instead of the track, in each specific area of their life.

Coaching Prompt

For each domain below, name the subject, the specific outcome or habit you want your lens on, and the wall, the specific fear, distraction, or obstacle currently pulling your eyes away from it.

Be precise. Not "I get distracted at work," but the actual wall, a specific fear of a conversation, a specific app, a specific worry that hijacks your attention.

2
The Root — Tracing Why Your Eyes Default to the Wall
A four part root-cause trace. Take your time here.

Choose the one domain from Exercise 1 costing you the most right now. This is the one you'll work through for the rest of this worksheet.

2a — The Modeled Pattern
Coaching Prompt

Growing up, or earlier in your adult life, who did you watch respond to obstacles by fixating on them rather than moving past them? What did that look like?

This could be a parent, a mentor, an early boss, or a version of yourself in a specific season. Behavior teaches more than advice does.

2b — The Formative Moment
Coaching Prompt

When did you first learn that keeping your eyes locked on a threat felt safer than keeping them on your goal? Was there a specific moment when looking away from danger, real or perceived, actually cost you something?

Take your time. This might be one sharp memory, or a slow accumulation across many smaller moments.

2c — The Inherited Belief
Coaching Prompt

If the modeled pattern and the formative moment were compressed into a single sentence, a rule about attention and danger you've been unconsciously obeying, what would that sentence be?

Write it exactly as it would sound out loud. It may never have been said to you directly. It may simply be the rule you wrote for yourself after watching or living through what you named above.

2d — The Cost Tally
Coaching Prompt

What has obeying this belief actually cost you, in this specific domain, across the time you've carried it?

Include missed progress, relationships strained by distraction, energy spent watching instead of building, or opportunities that quietly passed while your eyes stayed on the wall.

Notice ThisThe belief you just named was very likely a reasonable adaptation once, formed to keep you safe with the information and power you had at the time. Naming it precisely is not self-criticism. It's the first honest step toward retraining where your eyes go now.

3
The Diagnosis — External Wall or Internal Wall
Naming plainly whether the pull comes from your environment or from inside you.

This diagnosis determines which path you'll build in Part Two, and getting it right changes which tools will actually work.

Coaching Prompt

Is the wall you named in Exercise 1 primarily something in your environment, a notification, a person, a noisy schedule, or is it primarily something inside you, a fear, an old belief, a worry that hijacks your attention even in a quiet room?

Many people have both. Name whichever one has more control over this specific domain right now.

"You will always move toward whatever you keep your eyes on. Focus was never about the absence of the wall. It's about where you choose to look while it's still right there."

5
The Words — Drafting Your Redirect Phrase
Preparing the exact sentence for the moment your eyes lock onto the wall.

Tony Robbins, an expert on focus, still couldn't pull his own eyes off the wall by instinct. His instructor had to physically turn his head. You won't always have someone there to do that for you, which is why this phrase needs to be ready in advance.

Coaching Prompt

What is the one short sentence that reminds you, the instant you catch your eyes on the wall, to turn back toward the track, without needing to feel calm first?

Keep it short enough to say in one breath. You will need to use it in moments too fast for a long explanation.

Say it out loud once before moving on. It should feel almost automatic to repeat, not something you have to think through each time.

Part Three

Pressure Test — Testing Your System Against Real Life

A focus system survives easily on a calm afternoon. It doesn't survive as easily against an urgent interruption, a resurfacing fear, or someone questioning your choice to narrow your attention, unless you've already thought it through.

6
Scenario One — The Urgent Interruption
When something genuinely time-sensitive pulls at your deep work.
The Scenario

You're thirty minutes into focused work on your subject, and something arrives that feels genuinely urgent, a message, a call, a notification that could be important. It would be easy to justify checking it "just this once."

This scenario tests whether your blur or redirect system survives real urgency, not just idle temptation.
7
Scenario Two — The Old Fear Resurfaces
When a string of focused days is broken by the inherited belief returning.
The Scenario

You've had several genuinely focused days. Then the exact inherited belief from Exercise 2c resurfaces, telling you it's careless or dangerous to keep your eyes off the wall for this long.

This scenario tests whether you can recognize the old belief in a new outfit, rather than obeying it automatically again.
8
Scenario Three — Someone Questions Your Narrow Focus
When your commitment to one subject looks like you're missing out.
The Scenario

Someone close to you notices how narrowly you're focusing lately, and questions it, calling it limiting, or pointing out other opportunities you're "missing" by staying this focused.

This scenario tests whether you can hold your chosen subject without needing everyone else's approval of your blur.
Part Four

Protect — Making This a Practice, Not a Moment

One good focused week is a good start. It is not the whole shift. Part Four turns this worksheet into an ongoing practice, so your eyes don't quietly drift back to the wall for months before you notice.

9
The Weekly Practice — Building the Habit
The simple, recurring practice that catches drift early, plus a relapse plan.

The goal is not one focused week. The goal is building the habit of noticing quickly when your eyes have drifted back to the wall, in whatever domain you're working on.

  • Daily: Before starting your subject each day, say your redirect phrase from Exercise 5 out loud once.
  • Within 7 Days: Put your blur or redirect system from Exercise 4 into place, with the accountability mechanism you named.
  • Every Week: Ask yourself one question. "Where did my eyes actually spend most of their time this week, the subject or the wall?"
  • Every Month: Revisit your Domain Scan from Exercise 1 and your inherited belief from Exercise 2c. Has a new wall started forming elsewhere?
  • Whenever You Catch Yourself Mid-Distraction: Use that exact moment as proof the system is working, not evidence that it failed. Noticing is the win.

Before You Close This

Tony Robbins had every piece of knowledge he needed, and his eyes still locked onto the wall the instant the car pivoted. His instructor had to physically turn his head. The wall never moved. What changed was where his attention was pointed, and that redirection had to happen again and again before it became automatic.

A wedding photographer can't stop guests from moving through the frame. What they control is where the lens is pointed, and how much weight everything else is allowed to carry. That is the whole of this worksheet, applied to your actual life.

Living in Full Expression means building the practiced skill of choosing your subject and blurring the rest, over and over, especially in the exact moments it feels most counterintuitive to look away from the wall.

Before you close this session — write the one thing you now know, that you did not know when you opened it.

The wall will still be there tomorrow. You now know exactly what it is, exactly where you learned to stare at it, and exactly what your redirect looks like. The only thing left is practicing the turn.

Eyes on the track, not the wall.
